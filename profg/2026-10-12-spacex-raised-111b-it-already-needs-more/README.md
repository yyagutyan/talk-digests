# SpaceX Raised $111B — It Already Needs More

*What SpaceX's $40 billion borrowing plan means for its valuation, how "annoyance" friction works as a business strategy, and why OpenAI's math results don't yet make a business case.*

Source: [SpaceX Raised $111B — It Already Needs More](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9SAgLMahZm4) · Ed Elson, John Foley and Ronan Farrow · Prof G Markets · 36 min

## TL;DR

SpaceX is reportedly seeking $40 billion in bonds and bank loans to buy Nvidia chips and build data centers, and its bonds already trade at 85 cents on the dollar. John Foley of the Financial Times reads the plan as early, dependent on Musk delivering, and the $2.3 trillion valuation as resting on faith rather than earnings. Ronan Farrow argues that deliberate friction, such as hard cancellations and phone trees, screens out the people who most need help, and states and courts are starting to push back. The host also doubts that OpenAI's reported math breakthroughs can justify its $1.5 trillion valuation.

## Borrowing to Build Data Centers Faster Than the Cash Arrives

[▶ 00:01:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9SAgLMahZm4&t=87s)

SpaceX is reportedly seeking $40 billion to buy Nvidia chips: $30 billion in investment-grade bonds and $10 billion in bank loans. That follows an $86 billion IPO four months ago and a $25 billion bond sale less than two weeks after it. Those bonds now trade at 85 cents on the dollar. Investors reportedly received a two-page memo with pictures of outer space and an arrow pointing to data centers "somewhere in the universe."

John Foley, head of the Lex column at the Financial Times, explains the math. Musk wants 10 gigawatts of data centers by the end of next year. At a rough $50 billion per gigawatt, that is about $500 billion. SpaceX has roughly $90 billion on hand. The payoff case is that data center rents are high enough to recover costs fast; the company's CFO has said payback can come in under a year. The bet is that SpaceX builds faster than rivals while demand for capacity stays strong.

Foley reads the memo as early, "back of the cocktail napkin" communication rather than final documentation. He expects a more concrete deal when the issue closes next year, still resting on the idea that data centers in space are viable, which many scientists doubt.

**Analyst research:** Foley says sell-side research is hard to trust given the fees at stake. Many price targets extrapolate next year's revenue and apply a multiple, which he does not find rigorous. He suspects many analysts "started with the answer and worked their way backwards."

**Credit signals:** The cost of insuring SpaceX debt rose 294 basis points on Wednesday, from 110 basis points in June. Foley reads the 50-year bonds (near 85 cents) and 5-year bonds (near 95) as awareness of heavier borrowing, not desperation. Revenue is expected to quadruple by the end of 2028, which would make the debt look less alarming. Losing its investment-grade rating would sharply raise yields, and Foley says the rating depends on Musk delivering and on Starlink's strength.

**What the company is now:** Starlink is profitable and dominant in connectivity, but capital spending has shifted to data centers, over $18 billion last quarter. The IPO filing cited a roughly $23 trillion addressable market, $22 trillion of it enterprise apps, where SpaceX has little revenue today. Renting data centers to AI labs is where the revenue is now, but it is early-stage. Foley says Mars remains the long-term story.

**The stock:** At about $167 a share and a $2.3 trillion market cap, the stock is up from an August low of $108. Foley scored his own IPO predictions: it would explode (right), then be cut in half (right), then slide as lockups expired (wrong). He credits the rally partly to Starship's first orbital flight and partly to a loyal investor base, as with Tesla. He says the valuation rests on faith in Musk, not on earnings. The float is small and the company is missing from some major indices, so the price reflects weak price discovery. Still, it sets a reference point for valuing companies such as Anthropic and OpenAI.

## Friction as a Business Strategy

[▶ 00:19:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9SAgLMahZm4&t=1191s)

Ronan Farrow, a Pulitzer-winning New Yorker journalist, says the **annoyance economy** (phone trees, hidden fees, hold times, hard-to-cancel subscriptions) is a deliberate business practice. Amazon gave its Prime cancellation path the code name "Iliad," and cancellations fell 14% after launch. A Stanford economist found that a difficult cancellation process can raise revenue anywhere from 14% to more than 200%, depending on the product. Ticketing firms, hotels, airlines, cable and phone companies, banks and health insurers are frequent offenders.

Farrow argues friction screens out the people who most need help. In 2024 the CFPB sued Comerica, alleging its vendors dropped more than 24 million calls before anyone picked up, as a matter of policy. Comerica ran the debit card many people used to receive Social Security. By some counts, up to a quarter of insured Americans under 65 report skipping care because of paperwork.

Critics allege the same logic drives government programs. Under the latest federal budget, low-income Medicaid adults must document work and re-prove eligibility every six months instead of yearly, and food stamps face the same treatment. Arkansas's 2018 paperwork tightening cost 18,000 people Medicaid, most of them eligible. The CBO projects 5.3 million more uninsured by 2034, about 2.8 million of them losing coverage over paperwork rather than failing the work test.

States are moving first:
- California requires advertised prices to include all mandatory fees, and cancellation must match the signup method.
- New York City's rule, effective October 1, carries large fines for not allowing click-to-cancel.

Courts are acting too: Amazon settled with the FTC for $2.5 billion, StubHub agreed to pay $10 million in fees, and a New York court shut down Sirius XM's cancellation maze. The FTC's national click-to-cancel rule was thrown out on a technicality. Farrow says consumer protection has been hollowed out federally, and the host notes that protecting consumers is the FTC's job.

Farrow sees a rare bipartisan issue, so he suggests contacting representatives. He also urges pushing back: few people appeal. In a lawsuit against UnitedHealth, 0.2% of patients appealed denied claims, and about 90% of those appeals won. As he puts it, "it pays to be a squeaky wheel."

## Breakthrough Math, Shaky Business Case

[▶ 00:32:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9SAgLMahZm4&t=1939s)

OpenAI released hundreds of unverified proofs, claiming progress on problems including the Riemann hypothesis. Rutgers' Alex Kontorovich says a human doing this would instantly win a Fields Medal.

The host sees real science but doubts the business case:

- Research is usually unprofitable.
- Being good at math doesn't make a good business.
- OpenAI's losses are growing, yet it seeks a $1.5 trillion valuation, twice Walmart's, with under a tenth of Walmart's revenue.
