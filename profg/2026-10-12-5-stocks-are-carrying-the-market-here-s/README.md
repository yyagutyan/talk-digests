## Anthropic's losses, customer concentration, and a $2 trillion ask

[▶ 00:05:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OvpLb3512UQ&t=338s)

Anthropic's S1 shows revenue grew 12x to nearly $4.6 billion last year, while operating losses widened to more than $8 billion. Scott says the losses are very large, though smaller than OpenAI's, and the company is reportedly set to go public soon, possibly after the midterms.

**Customer concentration** is the main worry. Paul Kedrosky noted that about a quarter of revenue comes from two clients. Scott, who has run B2B services firms, says that kind of concentration can be acceptable for a small company but not for one asking for a $2 trillion valuation. He contrasts it with Meta, which has about 300,000 clients and none above a third of a percent. Scott cites Ramp spending data showing that roughly 80% of enterprise revenue at both OpenAI and Anthropic comes from 1% of customers. He compares this to *Mad Men*, where Sterling Cooper's business collapsed when Lucky Strike left.

**The valuation** is the second problem. A $2 trillion price would exceed Meta, Saudi Aramco, Broadcom, Berkshire Hathaway, Eli Lilly and JP Morgan. Scott says there is no broad AI bubble, since Nvidia's valuation has already come down, but there is an **AI lab bubble**. He argues the price is not pricing in the risks.

Scott's math on growth:
- At roughly 435 times trailing revenue, the multiple falls to about 7 if revenue grows 10x again next year.
- He says the valuation assumes 74% annual growth for ten years, which no company has sustained, Nvidia included.
- The math implies the entire AI market must nearly quadruple for Anthropic alone to hit its number.
- Growth is driven by a few huge spenders, mainly Alphabet and Amazon.

**Tail risks** Scott lists include a rogue AI causing traffic-signal chaos, and the FTC investigation into whether OpenAI and Anthropic misled customers about harms. Axios reporting cites thousands of security breaches, with no legal action so far. He also points to four upcoming films about tech founders, including a Musk documentary, a Holmes documentary and *Artificial* about Sam Altman, which could affect product usage. Scott wants the price to reflect these risks: "Let's reduce the price a little bit."

Scott says he has told people who own the stock to sell. His own exposure was indirect, through FTX bankruptcy claims that have since been sold. He calls himself not a permabear and says he owns Meta, Microsoft and SaaS stocks.

**OpenAI's Dots** is a consumer assistant for everyday tasks such as booking reservations and doctor's appointments. The hosts think the direction is right. CFO Sarah Friar accidentally called it "Muse" on TV, and the live demo had errors. The hosts say the bigger problem for OpenAI is the FTC probe. One host jokes that a plush-toy design is sandbagging, though it is smarter business than a menacing alien mascot.

## Shelved IPOs, a narrow rally, and where to put money as yields rise

[▶ 00:30:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OvpLb3512UQ&t=1845s)

Six companies have postponed IPOs this quarter, including Oura and SB Energy, and most cite market uncertainty. Scott says he was caught flat-footed: he had predicted Oura would be a successful IPO. He thinks the delays come from a gap between bankers and management over valuation. Reporting said one of Oura's largest investors wanted to sell its entire stake, which sent a bad signal. Scott expects some companies to wait for a frothier market, and he reads the delays as investors with discipline, not a crisis.

The rally is narrow. Over the past month, more than half of S&P 500 companies have fallen 20% or more, and only information technology rose. Five stocks (Microsoft, Meta, Apple, Alphabet and Nvidia) account for 93% of the index's gains since July. Scott says valuations outside those names are coming down, which he sees as healthy.

Rates are the main pressure. The 10-year yield hit 5.3%, its highest since 2002, and the 30-year passed 5.6%. Ed notes that higher yields raise the return stocks must beat, which usually lowers valuations. Inflation is still sticky at 3.4% against a 2% target, even after a better-than-expected report. The green flags are growth above 2%, unemployment under 4.5%, booming profits, and valuations falling because earnings grow faster than prices.

Ed thinks a correction could happen this year, and he says it is time to buy bonds. He calls the sell-off warranted in part, citing fiscal concerns, but overdone. Close to 6% on Treasuries is, in his words, "great insurance" against downside risk. Scott agrees that fixed income is paying again, with some fairly good companies yielding about 8%, but he says not to try to be a hero. He suggests a low-cost fund such as one from Vanguard, and he doesn't expect yields to climb much further. Scott also cites the common rule of thumb of 60% equities and 40% bonds, shifting toward fixed income with age.

On selling, Scott's lesson is that market dynamics trump individual performance:

- When business is strong, your instinct to hold is usually wrong. Acquirers pay for growth, and that is when to sell.
- Scott turned down offers of 10 to 20 times revenue in the late 1990s, then sold Profit in 2000 for 2.8 times revenue after the market had changed.
- Good results are partly the market, and bad results are partly the industry being out of favor.

Ed's closing point is that understanding the market is part of running a business, since "the music" determines the price you get.

## Apple trims layers, but the stock still needs a new product

[▶ 00:56:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OvpLb3512UQ&t=3361s)

Apple's new CEO, one month in, is reportedly considering moving away from the traditional spring and fall launch schedule and has cut some middle-management roles. The goal is fewer layers between engineers and senior executives, with reported cuts at Siri, Vision Pro and the hardware division. The stock fell nearly 3% on the news, then rose after Bloomberg reported a bigger smart-home push: a hub due in mid-October that controls thermostats and door locks and supports FaceTime and intercom-style calls.

Galloway praises the cost discipline. Rarely do new CEOs cut costs instead of chasing growth, and at this size, "it doesn't have a growth problem, it has a metabolism problem." Layers add risk aversion, which creates openings for disruptors. He expects the home to be a winner.

Elson is skeptical of the valuation. Apple trades at about 38 times earnings against a five-year average of 30, and grew 16% last quarter, while Nvidia grew more than 100%. He says the stock needs a "golden goose," such as a new iPhone, and he won't buy until one appears. He is more open to the smart-home hub than to the other products he named.

## Apple's late entry into the smart home

[▶ 01:03:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OvpLb3512UQ&t=3810s)

Galloway predicts Apple will enter the home with a hub: a speaker plus a smart-home control panel centered on Siri. He would retire the Siri brand, which he calls a "total disaster."

His case is that Apple wins by arriving late. It didn't invent the MP3 player, smartphone, smartwatch or wireless earbuds, but it captured the profit pool. Few great brands sit in the home, and Apple is the least distrusted on privacy, which matters against Amazon and Meta. Amazon proved demand but not a business: its devices unit has lost tens of billions, while Apple sells hardware at a profit. Apple's 2 billion-plus active devices give it distribution, and an October 13 announcement lands in holiday season.

Elson disagrees. Privacy is the only edge he sees, and he doesn't care about Face ID on a speaker or an Apple lock. "I'm good with keys."

Galloway's verdict: "Amazon built the house. Apple's going to move in and charge rent."

## Apple's hub as the household AI gateway
