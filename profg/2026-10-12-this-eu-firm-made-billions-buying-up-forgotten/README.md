# This EU Firm Made Billions Buying Up Forgotten Tech

*How Bending Spoons buys struggling software companies, rebuilds them with small teams and AI, and how it hires.*

Source: [This EU Firm Made Billions Buying Up Forgotten Tech](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc) · Ed Elson and Luca Ferrari · Prof G Markets · 1 h 6 min

## TL;DR

Bending Spoons buys struggling software companies such as Vimeo, AOL, Airtable and Eventbrite, then cuts costs and rebuilds them on its own platform. Co-founder and CEO Luca Ferrari says its model is a hybrid of private equity and software, with headcount cuts as one lever among several. Its growth has come almost entirely from acquisitions, funded mainly by reinvested cash flow and debt. The approach matters because it shows a turnaround strategy built on engineering rigor, data-driven hiring and AI, and it was built from Europe.

## Key takeaways

- Bending Spoons has never sold a material business: it buys companies to hold and operate them indefinitely.
- Unlike typical private equity, it rebuilds acquired organizations and rearchitects their cloud infrastructure, then moves them onto its own platform within a few months of closing.
- Headcount cuts, often 80% or more, are a means to lower costs and rebuild small teams in startup mode, and cost efficiency drives about 50% of value creation.
- Acquisition screens: target companies that are large relative to Bending Spoons' size, have predictable performance five or six years out, and have clear room for major improvement.
- Hiring is measured against an objective function: how colleagues rate a new hire's performance after 12 months, with signals such as GPA being only weak predictors on their own.
- Bending Spoons tracks candidates only before hiring and then gives staff one year-end review, choosing trust over surveillance.
- AI is used both for new features, such as better recommendations for Eventbrite, and for internal operations, such as Slack agents that fix bugs, with open-weight models handling about 99% of tasks.
- Ferrari's advice for founders is to look for overlooked, unglamorous problems rather than chasing areas where big players already spend billions, and to try before giving up.

## A turnaround model that went public

[▶ 00:01:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=100s)

Bending Spoons buys struggling software companies, including Vimeo, AOL, Airtable and Eventbrite, then overhauls how they run, cutting costs to make them profitable again. Over the summer it listed on NASDAQ at a valuation above $18 billion, and its stock rose 40% on the first day of trading. Co-founder and CEO Luca Ferrari joins to explain how the model works.

## A failed startup that seeded Bending Spoons

[▶ 00:03:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=186s)

In 2010, Ferrari and co-founders launched an AI diary-writing startup in Copenhagen, which he calls too early. After three years and $1 million raised, it failed to scale. Investors sold their shares back for $1; the founders liquidated the company and recovered about $40,000. That seeded Bending Spoons in 2013. Ferrari says the strategy and key insights came from that failure.

## Buying Products From Tired Founders, and the Name

[▶ 00:04:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=280s)

After a failed startup, the founders had $40,000 left. Strong startups often failed to find **product-market fit**, so their plan was to buy products from founders who were tired of running them or lacked scaling skills.

A diversified portfolio needed a product-neutral name. Co-founder Matteo, a Matrix fan, proposed Bending Spoons, a nod to the mind's power and some self-mockery.

## Acquisitions from the start, not a studio model

[▶ 00:07:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=432s)

Its first acquisition, in 2014, cost $10,000. Compounding has since grown that into a nearly $4 billion revenue run rate.

In every year, at least 90% of revenue has come from acquired businesses. The "studio" image comes from Evernote, its first well-known deal.

Early products were built from scratch because there was no money. Launches could move the needle, but acquisitions were the core.

Growth came mainly from reinvested cash flow and debt. Equity came only recently, with a quarter-billion-dollar injection in late 2025.

## Buying an app for its users, then fixing it

[▶ 00:09:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=579s)

Ferrari's first acquisition was an iOS app that personalized the iPhone keyboard, around 2014, after Apple opened keyboard APIs. Bending Spoons bought it for its users and App Store position despite almost no monetization. They rewrote the software, redesigned the UI and added monetization. Core rule: beat the seller at the functional expertise the business needs; the bar is higher today.

## A hybrid of private equity and software

[▶ 00:11:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=687s)

Ferrari says Bending Spoons grows mainly through acquisitions, like private equity, but differs in three ways:

- It buys to hold and operate forever. It has never sold a material business.
- Private equity usually makes superficial changes with small financial teams. Bending Spoons rebuilds the organization, often completely, and rearchitects the cloud infrastructure. Its roughly 800-person core team is mostly engineers, data scientists, product managers and designers.
- It integrates acquisitions deeply onto one platform. Within a few months of closing, it replaces the acquired company's technology with dozens of in-house components, from AI orchestration to payments.

Ferrari calls the model "a bit of a hybrid." The downside is that selling one of its businesses would be nearly impossible.

## Headcount cuts as a means, not the goal

[▶ 00:17:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=1040s)

Ferrari confirms that headcount cuts are a consistent pattern, but says cost is only part of the goal. Cost efficiency drives about 50% of value creation, and organisational optimisation is a little over half of that, roughly 30–35% of the total.

He argues headcount barely predicts innovation. Talent density, powerful tools, culture and low red tape matter more. Bending Spoons cuts staff, often by 80% or more, and rebuilds small, talented teams in "startup mode." He reports faster bug fixes, fewer technical incidents and quicker launches.

Layoffs remain painful, he admits, so the company offers transparency, top-of-market separation packages and flexibility. He respects people who disagree with the approach on moral grounds.

## Three tests for an acquisition

[▶ 00:22:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=1321s)

Many targets are struggling, though not all. Bending Spoons screens for three things:

1. **Large relative to its size:** transformation effort doesn't scale linearly, so about five large deals a year beat many small ones.
2. **Predictable performance** five or six years out.
3. **Clear room for major improvement.**

Among companies passing all three, Ferrari says the firm picks the highest-return deals so it can compound as fast as possible.

## Old brands as a byproduct, not a plan

[▶ 00:25:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=1503s)

Mostly a byproduct, Ferrari says; there's no guideline to seek old brands. In recent years, he says, markets undervalued unglamorous businesses and overvalued new, sexy ones.

Comparing returns after screening and math, flashier targets consistently did worse. AOL isn't obviously going places, yet about 30 million people use it as their main email; they're no less valuable customers.

## Engineering minds applied to value investing

[▶ 00:27:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=1634s)

Ferrari and his co-founder studied engineering physics and bring that science mindset to business, which offers hard problems and good returns. Ferrari argues STEM trains problem-solving that makes finance easy to learn. They study Buffett, Tom Murphy and Benjamin Graham, without copying blindly. Their value approach is math: markets overprice prime businesses, so they buy past-prime ones to compound efficiently.

## Bending Spoons vs. Berkshire Hathaway: Shared Mindset, Different Control

[▶ 00:30:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=1809s)

Ferrari sees parallels with Berkshire Hathaway: a forever-owner mindset and rational investing. He calls the comparison apt but says, humbly, that they've done about 1% of what Buffett's firm did. Berkshire is a passive acquirer that lets companies run. Bending Spoons takes the opposite approach, making big changes and folding acquisitions into its technology and people platform.

## Operating from Europe, with a US-heavy business

[▶ 00:32:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=1924s)

Though formally European, Bending Spoons is largely American: over 50% of staff and about 60% of revenue are in the US. R&D and headquarters remain in Italy.

Milan was a deliberate choice. Ferrari cites Italy's 60 million people, good education, and talented people with few strong job options, a win-win for them and the company. He also wants to show that a global-caliber tech company can be built outside hubs like Silicon Valley.

The downside is regulation. Ferrari says permits are hard to get and underperformers hard to fire. He backs a generous welfare state, funded by taxes rather than by burdening companies. Europe, he says, could be much better.

## Why talent and ambition drift to the US

[▶ 00:36:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=2171s)

Ferrari cites regulation and culture as causes of Europe's productivity gap. Europe tends to fear failure and distrust success, often blaming luck or help. Fragmented markets slow winners. Small frictions compound as resources drift to more appealing places. He estimates three in four of Italy's top 100 graduates end up in the US, often before graduating.

## Brain drain: a company problem, a societal one

[▶ 00:38:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=2324s)

Ferrari says talent leaving Europe is not a management problem for Bending Spoons, since it brings people back. Europeans who miss family get high pay and fast career growth. It also hires students who would otherwise go abroad. Still, he calls the wider brain drain a massive societal problem with no silver bullet; real reform would take decades to reverse.

## Treating job offers as the product

[▶ 00:40:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=2447s)

Ferrari says job offers are Bending Spoons' most important product, ahead of any customer-facing one. Leaders should stand in candidates' shoes: pay, autonomy, trust and respect.

Early on, recruiting is persuasion. At 50 people, three or four staff only recruited. Credible vision and energy win over the convincible.

Hires then bring in friends. Glassdoor reviews now let candidates check the claims.

Selection is data work: data scientists and AI researchers hunt for signals that predict performance.

## Hiring against a measurable outcome

[▶ 00:44:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=2672s)

Bending Spoons starts with an **objective function**: how colleagues rate a hire's performance after 12 months on the job. Hiring several hundred people a year lets them search deep in the funnel and still reach statistical significance.

They then track many signals beyond the application and find which combinations predict that outcome. The statistics are well established. Capturing the 12-month outcome reliably is the harder part.

Individual signals are mostly weak:

- GPA is a mild predictor, and a selective university is weaker still.
- Being politically active as a teenager but not in your 20s predicts drive and entrepreneurship.
- How candidates write to support staff who cannot hire them predicts whether they will be humble, collaborative teammates.

Combined, the signals are fairly predictive. New ones keep turning up, many from practical tests.

## Trust over surveillance

[▶ 00:48:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=2894s)

Unlike Ray Dalio's Bridgewater, which drew criticism for tracking every interaction, Bending Spoons tracks candidates only before hiring. After that, staff get one year-end review based on colleague input.

Ferrari says people do their best when trusted, so the company hires people it believes can excel and gives them "uncomfortably high" trust. Monitoring would destroy that.

Performance is judged by outcomes over time, not by how work got done, including AI use or late hours.

## Using AI to make acquired businesses more effective

[▶ 00:52:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=3148s)

Ferrari describes two uses. The first is features that only recent AI makes possible, such as better recommendations for Eventbrite's roughly 80 million monthly visitors. Eventbrite has far less data than Meta, but he says even marginal gains matter to attendees and organizers.

The second is operational efficiency. Each "Alt Spooner" is a Slack agent with its user's access. An Evernote general manager had one find a bug's root cause and submit a fix for review in under five minutes of her time. Ferrari estimates that work took about 10 hours two years ago, and says the bug likely would never have been prioritized.

An in-house gateway routes each task to the best model mix for quality and cost. Open-weight models handle about 99% of tasks at near-zero cost, while frontier models only supervise, like a senior engineer overseeing junior engineers.

Bending Spoons reports nearly $4B in run-rate revenue, 94% of its code written with AI, and about $15M in annualized AI spend.

## Whether AI will take jobs

[▶ 00:57:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=3479s)

Ferrari agrees the host's point holds and says he is very concerned. Until recently he expected most people to be unemployed within 10 years.

He calls "AI will create jobs because it can't beat humans at everything" a dumb argument: brains are physical, so the gap will close.

He finds an economist's **comparative advantage** argument more convincing. AI is limited by energy and chips, so humans keep tasks AI shouldn't use resources on, and productivity gains could raise their pay.

Best case: AI wealth is shared, freeing people for hobbies. Worst case: control of AI is lost and energy limits drive a dystopia. He is excited as a technologist but scared.

## Find overlooked problems and commit to trying

[▶ 01:02:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdmnHQmG8vc&t=3759s)

For AI founders, look for value where few people are looking: unseen, unsexy niches where you can ask what AI could improve. Chasing where big players already spend billions is a losing bet; you're probably not Elon Musk. Entrepreneurship, like having children, is rarely regretted even after failure. Don't be stubborn: if you keep failing, get a job, but try first.
