# Building AIE: Open Q&A and Feedback Session with swyx

*How the AI Engineer organizer handles safety talks, expo length, workshop overflow, Wi-Fi, the app and speaker selection, and what changes for next year.*

Source: [Building AIE: Open Q&A and Feedback Session with swyx](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70) · swyx · AI Engineer · 59 min

## TL;DR

At an open Q&A at the 2026 World's Fair, swyx, an AI Engineer organizer, took audience feedback on the conference's programming, logistics and app. Most complaints came from things that don't scale: overfull workshop rooms, congested Wi-Fi, a thin new-engineer orientation and a weak vendor app. swyx's answers mostly hold the format steady, including the same Moscone size, no AI safety talks in the program, and an in-house app next year. This matters to engineers because it explains how talks are chosen from about 2,200 applications for roughly 300 spots, and how partner events and local meetups are meant to grow the community.

## Key takeaways

- swyx keeps AI safety and X-risk talks out of the AI Engineer program because the conference focuses on what attendees use at work, and he points to the security, sandboxing and evals tracks as where safety is already covered.
- The World's Fair stays at Moscone at the same size, venue and week next year, with existing space used better because some rooms sat empty, and growth will come through partner events in other countries.
- Workshop rooms are roughly equal in size but seats aren't allocated, so if a room fills, move to your second or third choice; pre-registration slipped to next year after a paid app vendor couldn't make it work.
- About 40 countries have asked for their own AIE through the partnerships program; Bangalore comes first, and the planned partner events are Miami, Melbourne, Sydney, Paris and Singapore, with Shanghai undecided.
- For local meetups, keep the bar low: a fixed venue and time builds a habit, the usual format is two talks with social time, and solid organization and publishing matter more than sponsors, websites or YouTube channels.
- Next year the vendor conference app is dropped in favor of the web app, and the underlying JSON data will be published, which already has MCP and CLI access.
- Speaker selection is very competitive, with about 2,200 applications for roughly 300 spots; applications aren't blind, so submit prior talks even if they have few views, and tie your work to a broader trend.
- Breakouts stay at 20 minutes, which swyx thinks is enough given shrinking online attention though he admits it could go either way, and this year AIE ran its first poster sessions for students and academics.

## An open feedback session and next year's events

[▶ 00:00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=0s)

[swyx](https://x.com/swyx), one of the organizers, opened an open-ended Q&A on how the conference is run. He said the team takes feedback seriously and applies it to the next conference. He also announced that the conference returns in 2027, and that a New York conference comes next, focused on finance and anything money and AI.

## Keeping AI safety talks out of the program

[▶ 00:01:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=62s)

Swyx keeps AI safety and X-risk talks out, since the conference covers what attendees use at work. Meetups cover them instead.

An audience member argues engineers should weigh where their systems lead.

Swyx replies that security, sandboxing and evals tracks already cover safety, and real-world evals like Vending-Bench surface more alignment issues than abstract X-risk talk.

## Is a four-day expo too much?

[▶ 00:03:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=221s)

An attendee said four days is too long and suggested two expo days plus one partner-focused day. swyx disagrees: the expo can stay open while sponsors pay and staff it. Unlike his curated summits, World's Fair is meant as an open expo, and many attend single days. He floated more expo space upstairs to ease talk-hopping pressure, though standing is tiring.

## Partner AIEs and fitting into the conference landscape

[▶ 00:06:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=397s)

AIE was inspired by Shift in Croatia, which swyx rates among the best he has spoken at. This year brings four to five partner AIEs: Miami, Melbourne, Sydney, Paris and Singapore, with Shanghai undecided. swyx is wary of asking for more travel and of AIE's place among other conferences: it is three years old yet now bigger than Shift.

## Partner-run AIE events, starting with Bangalore

[▶ 00:08:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=512s)

About 40 countries want their own AIE through the partnerships program on the website. Swyx likens it to TED versus TEDx: he won't limit local communities, but wants the brand to keep technically strong content. Bangalore comes first.

AIE has no community beyond social media. Swyx prefers meeting people where they already are: "I don't want to add yet another Slack to your already 200 Slacks." Local meetups get support.

## Running a local meetup on a shoestring

[▶ 00:11:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=707s)

swyx calls meetups a labor of love: speakers, venues and pizza are always last-minute, so volunteers help each other. A fixed venue and time builds a habit.

The norm is two talks with social time around them. Sponsors, websites and YouTube channels are extra work; solid organization and publishing cover most of it.

Keep the bar low. Running a full conference is a full-time job.

## Workshop rooms fill up and lines form

[▶ 00:15:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=920s)

Attendees queued for hours and still missed workshop rooms. The organizers admit they failed here. Rooms are roughly equal in size, but seats aren't allocated. Everything is recorded, so when a room fills, the advice is to move to your second or third choice. Pre-registration was planned for this year, but a paid app vendor couldn't make it work, so it slipped to next year. The organizers call the fix mostly a software problem.

## Holding the size at Moscone

[▶ 00:17:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=1032s)

Big events attract top speakers, but there is a limit. Next year stays the same size, venue and week, with existing space used better, since some rooms sat empty.

Swyx says sponsors, attendees, speakers and staff have now seen a World's Fair at Moscone. Growth waits until the event is good enough, and will come only through other countries. The event stays in San Francisco.

## Conference Wi-Fi congestion

[▶ 00:20:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=1202s)

Bad Wi-Fi broke even workshop demos and drew refund demands. swyx says organizers buy the maximum package offered every year, but most venues aren't built for engineers downloading gigabytes per laptop. This venue beats last year's basement, since everyone can tether. Speakers and sponsors have separate networks, but attendee congestion is expected. Buying their own hotspots is the next option, though swyx says that has never worked well at any conference.

## Unscheduled time for leaders versus full session slots

[▶ 00:23:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=1403s)

Alex asked for more free time for leaders to share experience.

swyx is not sure it's the top priority. Leaders can already meet in the leadership lounge or skip sessions, and he would rather fill slots with strong speakers, even if rooms are half empty, since talks sometimes do better online.

For summits, he points to London's leadership lunches: a brief guided discussion, then table chat on three set topics.

## Replacing the conference app with open data

[▶ 00:26:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=1583s)

An attendee asked for a location filter in the dedicated mobile app, such as hiding booths when browsing the agenda. swyx wants to drop that vendor app next year and ship only the web app. He resisted building his own app for years because the existing ones were poor. Next year he plans to publish the underlying JSON data, which already has MCP and CLI access, while still providing a good default.

## Why breakouts are 20 minutes and workshops run longer

[▶ 00:29:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=1770s)

An attendee found 20-minute breakouts too short for substance and preferred hour-long workshops.

Swyx said lengths were mixed: a workshop day with 10 tracks on day one, then three workshop rooms for longer sessions on the remaining days, modeled on GDC's one-hour format.

He argues shrinking online attention makes 20 minutes a good length, and feels it's enough, though it could go either way. He still wants people to go deep, but many who want more time don't get it.

## Sequencing talks for continuity versus standalone YouTube viewing

[▶ 00:31:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=1916s)

Every track is recorded and posted to YouTube. In past AIEs, swyx sequenced talks by hand, clustering related topics so they flowed into each other, since he knew every talk's content. Speaker availability often breaks that plan. swyx says standalone YouTube viewing is the format to optimize for. He builds a playlist per track to craft a narrative, though nobody uses them.

## Turning new-engineer orientation into real onboarding

[▶ 00:33:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=2024s)

An attendee found the New Engineer Orientation (NEO) too thin. It covered highlights but left questions about lunch and the daily schedule open.

The organizers say they underestimated the load of running NEO, and it became more networking than onboarding. An online attendee guide existed, but few people saw it, and not everyone reads the emails. NEO grew out of last year's feedback, and the team expects it to improve.

## Too many talks: FOMO as a sign you're being pushed

[▶ 00:36:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=2171s)

An attendee felt overwhelmed by parallel sessions and lost time with speakers afterward. The host calls this FOMO and anxiety, worth managing but a **"high-class problem"**: recordings stay on YouTube.

The summit is single-track, so attendees choose what to attend. The World's Fair is the big show for everyone's best work of the year.

Discomfort is expected. In the fastest-moving field, feeling fully comfortable means you're probably not being pushed.

## Attendee feedback on the conference app

[▶ 00:38:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=2339s)

The app got mixed reviews. The organizer said it "mostly worked" but logged these problems for next year:

- Android crashed repeatedly when reopening a talk.
- Returning from a talk showed a loading screen for several seconds, sometimes resetting scroll position. Loading also appeared while scrolling.
- My Schedule had no search or filters and started from day one, even for past sessions.
- Ongoing sessions couldn't be saved.
- The web and app don't sync.
- Many booths had no name or address.
- Talk chat and Q&A went unused and could be removed.
- The company filters loaded slowly.

swyx proposed a matching system to connect similar attendees, such as founders or big-tech staff. The leadership track prototyped one with researched attendee profiles, and swyx wants to invest more in it, having seen it work at other conferences. The current app is a purchased vendor product that will be replaced in-house. The organizer's own website was praised as easier to navigate, and this feedback will become next year's requirements. swyx noted that the app's data is open source, so attendees could build their own, and said "engineers deserve a good app."

## Team investment and a stronger leadership track

[▶ 00:44:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=2691s)

A nonprofit sports organization says it spends tens of thousands of dollars a year to send its team, treating the conference as an investment in its people. The team holds its own summit a month beforehand, rents a suite during the event, and spends each evening turning what it learned into concrete work.

The attendee said the leadership track has improved this year. Talks felt less salesy, and speakers were practitioners doing real work. swyx says the speakers came inbound because the conference is now established, and that he had little to do with it.

Ideas raised for the future: an unconference, which swyx worries wouldn't sell tickets without speakers, and a dedicated leadership retreat with off-the-record chats. swyx's priority remains: "content is king."

## Keeping the conference scrappy as it grows

[▶ 00:49:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=2942s)

Swyx worries that success brings suits. South by Southwest started scrappy, then sport coats replaced hoodies, and AIE may follow the same path.

His informal gauge is costumes: two at the first World's Fair, zero last year, nine this year. He wants AIE to stay a place where people can be themselves, like Comic-Con, with the suits kept on a separate floor.

## How to get selected to speak

[▶ 00:51:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=3108s)

Selection is very competitive: about 2,200 applications for roughly 300 spots, and some slots go to invited speakers. The vision track had only about 10 talks, so they tend to go first to teams with proprietary models and to well-known companies that draw attendees. Leftover slots go to lesser-known people.

swyx is on every committee, and trusted past speakers help vet and vote. Applications aren't blind, so submit prior talks, even recorded ones with few views. Unique work tied to a broader trend helps. The goal is to remove doubt that the talk will be strong, since the bar is high and the filter is imperfect.

## Poster sessions to bring academia to industry

[▶ 00:55:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snTZtttfL70&t=3325s)

This year AIE ran poster sessions for students and academics for the first time. swyx started AIE in opposition to academic conferences' early submission cutoffs, aiming to bring academia to industry. Selection was outsourced to a partner conference, and in-house peer review may follow. Because AIE is unaccredited, presenters can also present elsewhere without breaking exclusivity rules.
